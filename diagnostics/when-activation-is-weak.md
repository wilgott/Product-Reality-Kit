# When Activation Is Weak

Activation is not done when the customer signs.
Activation is when the customer starts using the product and reaches real value.
Use this diagnostic when customers buy or start onboarding, but do not get into production, do not start using the product, or do not reach value fast enough.

This is for B2B SaaS, onboarding-heavy products, workflow products, no-code products, and products that need setup, implementation, customer data, integrations, approvals, or internal customer resources before value appears.

Do not reduce activation to a welcome email, kickoff call, or completed checklist. Diagnose the whole path from signed customer to first value.

---

## When to use this

Use this when customers enter the funnel after buying, signing up, or starting onboarding, but do not reach first value.

Common signals:

- Customers sign, but never go live.
- Customers start onboarding, but do not finish.
- Customers complete onboarding tasks, but do not use the product.
- Customers use the product once, but do not return.
- Customers need manual help before they see any value.
- Setup takes longer than expected.
- Production use depends on integrations, data, approvals, or customer resources that do not arrive.
- Sales, CS, onboarding, and product disagree on what activation means.
- The team is discussing onboarding emails before reviewing the full onboarding journey.

---

## The uncomfortable truth

Weak activation usually means the value path is not operationally real enough.

The customer may not understand what was bought, what will be delivered, what they need to provide, who needs to be involved, or what first value should look like. Sales may have promised a faster or easier path than the product can support. Onboarding may be trying to compensate for unclear requirements, missing customer data, integration delays, product gaps, or a buyer/user mismatch.

When activation is weak, the easy story is "the customer is not engaged." Sometimes that is true. First check whether the customer was set up to succeed, whether the product asks too much before value appears, and whether your team has a clear enough process to get them live.

---

## First questions

Start here:

1. What does first value mean for this customer?
2. Does the customer understand what we deliver?
3. Does the customer understand what they need to do?
4. Are customer requirements clear?
5. Is the customer invested enough to get live?
6. Is onboarding set up to help them?
7. What blocks production?
8. Is setup too complex?
9. Are we waiting on customer data, integrations, approvals, or internal resources?
10. Did Sales set the right expectations?
11. Did buyer and user expect the same thing?
12. Are we trying to fix a process problem with onboarding emails?

---

## Red flags

Be careful when:

- Customers sign but do not use the product.
- Customers do not understand what they bought.
- Customers need manual help for every meaningful setup step.
- Requirements are unclear or discovered late.
- The customer is not engaged after the deal closes.
- Sales promised faster or easier setup than the team can deliver.
- CS or onboarding explains too much that the product, Sales process, or materials should have made clear.
- Too much configuration is required before value appears.
- Nobody agrees on what activation means.
- The team talks about onboarding emails before reviewing the full journey.
- The buyer is excited, but the day-to-day user does not see the value.
- Production use depends on customer resources that were never committed.

## Common false diagnoses

### "We just need better onboarding emails."

Maybe.

But emails cannot fix unclear requirements, missing data, integration blockers, weak handoffs, poor expectation setting, or a product that asks for too much setup before value.

Check first:

- Where customers actually stop.
- Whether they know what success looks like.
- Whether the required setup is reasonable.
- Whether the right customer roles are involved.
- Whether onboarding is compensating for a product, Sales, or implementation gap.

### "The customer is not engaged."

Maybe.

But low engagement may be a symptom, not the cause.

It may mean:

- The buyer did not involve the users.
- The customer never understood what they needed to provide.
- The customer cannot get data, approvals, integrations, or internal resources.
- Sales sold value, but not the work required to reach it.
- First value is too far away.
- The product does not make the next step clear.

### "The product is too hard."

Maybe.

But do not turn every activation problem into a broad product-quality verdict.

Before rebuilding, check:

- Which setup steps create the delay.
- Which steps require customer action.
- Which steps require your team.
- Which steps could be removed, defaulted, templated, or moved later.
- Whether the product is hard for everyone or only for customers outside the right fit.

---

## What to check

Check the full activation path before naming the failure:

- Time to first value.
- Time to production.
- Onboarding completion rate.
- Drop-off points by step, role, segment, and customer type.
- Customer requirements captured before kickoff.
- Sales promises about setup time, ease, data needs, integrations, and support.
- Customer engagement after signing or signup.
- Setup steps required before first value.
- Integration dependencies.
- Customer data readiness.
- Customer approvals, security reviews, procurement steps, and internal resourcing.
- CS and onboarding notes.
- Support questions during and after onboarding.
- Feature usage after onboarding.
- Whether the customer knows what success looks like.
- Whether buyer, admin, and end user expected the same outcome.
- Whether onboarding materials match the actual customer journey.
- Whether the onboarding team has the playbooks, access, product knowledge, and escalation paths needed to get customers live.

---

## Decision options

Possible decisions:

- Simplify onboarding.
- Improve requirement gathering before kickoff.
- Improve expectation setting in Sales.
- Create better onboarding material.
- Improve product setup.
- Reduce required configuration.
- Add templates, defaults, sample data, or prebuilt workflows.
- Improve the CS/onboarding handoff from Sales.
- Narrow ICP to customers who can reach value with the current setup path.
- Fix product gaps blocking first value.
- Review customer fit before accepting or prioritizing the account.
- Move nonessential setup steps after first value.
- Add a clearer activation owner and review cadence.
- Create a customer readiness checklist for data, integrations, approvals, and internal resources.

## Product Decision Record output

After the diagnostic, write a Product Decision Record.

Recommended template:

- [Standard PDR](../decision-records/product-decision-record.md)

Make sure the decision record answers:

- What does first value mean?
- Where are customers getting stuck?
- Is this product, onboarding, Sales, implementation, customer readiness, or a mix?
- What change reduces time to value?
- What are we saying no to?
- When will we review activation again?
- What evidence supports the decision?
- Who owns the next action?

## Simple rule

Activation is not a signed contract, completed signup, kickoff call, or onboarding email sequence.

Activation happens when the right customer reaches real value in production, with a path they understand and can repeat.
