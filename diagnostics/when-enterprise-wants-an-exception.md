# When Enterprise Wants an Exception

Enterprise customers can reveal where a segment is going.

They can also pull you into custom work that makes the product harder to sell, support, and maintain.

Use this diagnostic when a large customer asks for something that may pull the roadmap sideways.

---

## When to use this

Use this when you hear things like:

- "This customer is too important to lose."
- "They only need one special flow."
- "Can we make an exception?"
- "This is required for enterprise."
- "It will unlock a big contract."
- "We can hide it behind a setting."
- "It is not for everyone, just for them."

---

## The uncomfortable truth

Enterprise customers are not wrong for asking.

They have complex teams, complex workflows, procurement needs, compliance needs, and internal politics.

But every exception creates a cost somewhere.

The question is not:

> Is this customer important?

The better question is:

> Is this problem important for the market we want to serve?

---

## First questions

Start here:

1. What customer problem are they trying to solve?
2. Is this problem specific to this customer, or common in the segment?
3. Does this match our ideal customer profile?
4. Would other customers benefit from the same capability?
5. Is this a workflow problem, compliance need, permission issue, reporting gap, or procurement checkbox?
6. Is the requested solution the right solution?
7. Can we solve 80% of the problem in a generic way?
8. Does this make the product better, or just more flexible for one customer?
9. What will this cost to build, maintain, support, explain, and sell?
10. What would we stop doing if we say yes?

---

## Red flags

Be careful when:

- The solution only works for one customer.
- The request adds hidden complexity for future customers.
- Sales has already promised it.
- Engineering says it will complicate the core product.
- CS says it will be hard to support.
- The customer cannot explain the business outcome.
- The team calls it "enterprise" without knowing if other enterprise customers need it.
- The request sounds small but touches permissions, workflow logic, data model, reporting, billing, or compliance.

---

## Common false diagnoses

### "This is an enterprise requirement."

Maybe.

Or it may be one enterprise customer's internal process.

Do not confuse a customer process with a market requirement.

### "We can just hide it behind a setting."

A hidden setting still needs to be built, tested, documented, supported, and understood.

Settings do not remove complexity.

They often move complexity to the customer, CS, and Support.

### "The contract is big enough to justify it."

Maybe.

But revenue can make bad product decisions look good in the short term.

Ask what it does to the product over time.

---

## What to check

Before deciding, check:

- ARR or expansion potential
- Renewal risk
- Segment fit
- ICP fit
- Strategic fit
- Number of similar requests
- Support ticket patterns
- Sales notes from similar deals
- Compliance or audit requirements
- Implementation effort
- Long-term maintenance cost
- Whether the solution can be generalized
- Whether this should be product, service, or paid add-on

---

## Decision options

Possible decisions:

- Build it as a reusable product capability
- Build a smaller generic version
- Run discovery with similar customers
- Offer a workaround
- Make it a paid add-on
- Handle it as implementation/service work
- Put it in a later roadmap theme
- Say no and explain why
- Do nothing until there is stronger evidence

---

## Product Decision Record output

After the diagnostic, write a Product Decision Record.

Recommended template:

- [Standard PDR](../decision-records/product-decision-record.md)

Make sure the decision record includes:

- Whether this is a one-off or a market pattern
- What trade-off you are accepting
- Whether the solution is product, service, add-on, or no
- What you are saying no to
- When to review the decision again

## Simple rule

One enterprise customer is not a market.

But one enterprise customer can reveal a market.

Your job is to know the difference.
